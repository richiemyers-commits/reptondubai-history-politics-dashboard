Inside a Western Front trench

A British-sector trench system, c.1916

A composite reconstruction of a British-sector trench system around 1916. Depth, drainage, shelters and layouts differed with terrain, unit and date. The view combines features to explain how the system worked.

Illustration annotation key

  1. Parapet: The raised front edge faces the enemy.
  2. Sandbags: Packed bags reinforce exposed earthworks.
  3. Fire step: A raised ledge gives a view over the front.
  4. Revetment: Timber and other supports hold back soil.
  5. Duckboards: Wooden walkways keep feet above mud.
  6. Drainage channel: Water collects below the walkway.
  7. Sump: A low pit gathers water.
  8. Parados: A raised rear bank gives protection.
  9. Traverse: A bend interrupts the trench line.
  10. Communication trench: Links front and support areas.
  11. Support trench: Holds men and supplies behind the front.
  12. Dugout: A shelter cut into the earth.
  13. Bunks: Resting space is cramped and basic.
  14. Field telephone: Messages travel along wires.
  15. Observation point: A sentry watches the ground ahead.
  16. Barbed wire: Obstacles lie beyond the front parapet.
  17. No man’s land: Ground separates the opposing lines.
  18. Supply party: Food, water and equipment must arrive.
  19. Aid post: Initial care is provided behind the front.
  20. Latrine: Sanitation is a constant problem.

CHAPTER 1 OF 6

An imperial world at war

A European front sustained by global connections

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Make a table distinguishing the political relationships of India, Algeria, Canada and China.
  2. Explain why a soldier depends on labour behind the lines.
  3. Define “meaningful” using at least two criteria before examining the cases.

Empires and a world war

Britain and France drew on soldiers, workers and resources from overseas. India was under British rule; Algeria was under French colonial rule. Canada was a self-governing Dominion within the British Empire, while First Nations peoples faced colonial restrictions within Canada. These relationships were not identical.

The Western Front

Fighting across Belgium and northern France became associated with trenches, artillery, machine guns and attrition. Soldiers needed food, ammunition, transport and repairs as well as reinforcements. The original British Expeditionary Force suffered heavy losses in 1914; it was not simply wiped out at the Marne.

Scope of this enquiry

Our four cases are Ganga Singh and Indian troops, Algerian troops, Chinese labourers, and Mike Mountain Horse. Chinese labourers supported Britain and France, but China was not their colony and the workers were not front-line combat soldiers. Olusoga’s wider account extends beyond these cases and beyond Europe.

Vocabulary

  • Empire: territories and peoples under a power’s rule
  • Dominion: self-governing territory within the British Empire
  • Attrition: wearing an opponent down over time

Explain your thinking: Would a contribution have to win a battle by itself to be meaningful?

CHAPTER 2 OF 6

Ganga Singh & the Indian Army

Reinforcement and resistance at a critical moment

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Record a precise claim from the case and identify what its scale actually measures.
  2. Explain the chain from holding a position to wider military usefulness.
  3. Name one additional type of source you would seek.

The classroom case

The Ganga Singh sheet follows a soldier of the 57th Rifles during the First Battle of Ypres in 1914. Its vivid narrative describes pressure on Indian troops, unfamiliar equipment and determined resistance. Read it as a later teaching narrative: it is not a surviving diary written by Singh.

What contribution can we infer?

Indian troops reinforced a hard-pressed Allied front. Holding positions could buy time and help prevent a breakthrough. The case sheet states that the Indian Corps held ten miles of a twenty-five-mile British sector by the end of 1914. This indicates a substantial responsibility in that sector, not forty per cent of the whole Western Front.

Testing significance

Connect a defensive role to the wider need for time, manpower and secure positions. Avoid converting one dramatic personal story into proof that one soldier saved the war. Corroborate the narrative with unit records, wider deployments or other accounts; distinguish the individual from the Indian Corps as a whole.

Vocabulary

  • Reinforcement: additional troops or supplies
  • Sector: a defined section of a front
  • Corroboration: checking evidence against other evidence

Explain your thinking: How can a defensive action be meaningful even without a large advance?

CHAPTER 3 OF 6

Algerian troops at Ypres

Service, vulnerability and a new form of attack

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Locate Ypres in Belgium and distinguish the battles of 1914 and 1915.
  2. Explain why retreat in a gas attack is not a complete assessment of a unit.
  3. Separate evidence of sacrifice from evidence of military effect.

22 April 1915

At the Second Battle of Ypres, German forces released chlorine gas against Allied positions, including French and North African troops. Many soldiers lacked effective protection. The attack caused casualties and opened a gap in the line; other Allied troops helped prevent a decisive breakthrough.

A fair reading

The Algerian sheet focuses on the effects of gas and the soldiers’ distress. Retreat under an unfamiliar chemical attack cannot, by itself, prove cowardice or a lack of contribution. Ask about weapons, preparation, orders, neighbouring units and the tasks the troops had been assigned.

Judging rather than romanticising

Their presence demonstrates the French war effort’s imperial reach, but service and suffering are not identical to military effect. Establish the role of units and the context of the attack. Acknowledge sacrifice without assuming that casualties alone measure success, or that unequal treatment means their work did not matter.

Vocabulary

  • Colonial troops: troops recruited from territories under colonial rule
  • Chlorine gas: a chemical weapon used at Ypres in 1915
  • Salient: a section of a front projecting into enemy-held territory

Explain your thinking: Which evidence would you need before judging a unit’s performance fairly?

CHAPTER 4 OF 6

The Chinese Labour Corps

The work that made fighting possible

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Draw a chain from a tank repair to the ability to fight.
  2. Explain what the figure of about 140,000 includes.
  3. Compare one military consequence with one issue of treatment or recognition.

Workers, not colonial infantry

Britain and France recruited Chinese workers for support roles. Around 140,000 served the two powers in total; this figure is not the size of the British corps alone. French recruitment and the British Chinese Labour Corps were distinct arrangements. Chinese service must not be described as proof that China was a British or French colony.

The military value of labour

The classroom sheet describes digging, railway maintenance and repair work, including work on tanks at Erin. It connects intensive preparation with Cambrai in November 1917. Loading supplies, repairing equipment and maintaining routes could free others for combat and help armies operate. “Behind the lines” does not mean irrelevant or safe.

What can we conclude?

Labour’s effect can be indirect but essential. Build a chain from a particular task to an operational need, then test its scale. Workers also experienced segregation and discrimination, and many continued dangerous clearing work after the Armistice. Limited recognition is a separate question from the value of their labour.

Vocabulary

  • Logistics: movement and supply of people and equipment
  • Non-combatant: a person not serving in a direct fighting role
  • Segregation: enforced separation of groups

Explain your thinking: Can support work be essential without being the single decisive cause of victory?

CHAPTER 5 OF 6

Mike Mountain Horse

Individual service, identity and a remembered war

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Identify Mike’s Nation and the force in which he served.
  2. Compare the questions answered by a war robe and an army record.
  3. Explain the limits of generalising from one decorated soldier.

A Kainai soldier

Mike Mountain Horse was a member of the Kainai (Blood) Nation in Canada. His brother Albert served and died after damage from gas. The case describes Mike’s subsequent service in the Canadian Expeditionary Force and his continuing connection to his own community and traditions. Indigenous identity did not disappear inside a Canadian uniform.

Service and experience

Mike served on the Western Front, including at Cambrai, and received the Distinguished Conduct Medal. His story connects military service with bereavement, endurance and unequal colonial relationships. Avoid presenting one medal or one individual as a complete measure of every First Nations person’s experience.

Reading a different kind of source

After the war, Mike worked with Ambrose Two Chiefs to record selected experiences on a painted war robe. This offers a visual and cultural form of remembrance, shaped by choices about what mattered. Compare it with a service record: the sources can answer different questions and need not be judged by the same criteria.

Vocabulary

  • First Nations: Indigenous peoples in Canada, including the Kainai
  • Commemoration: remembering the past publicly or personally
  • Service record: official documentation of military service

Explain your thinking: What might an official record leave out that a personal memorial preserves?

CHAPTER 6 OF 6

Meaningful, decisive, remembered?

A judgement with criteria, scale and limits

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Rank your criteria, explaining your most important one.
  2. Build a comparison using at least three of the four cases.
  3. Give a qualified judgement that distinguishes meaningful from decisive.

Choose criteria before reaching a verdict

Assess manpower at a critical moment, the ability to hold or gain positions, and the labour that sustained operations. You might also consider scale, timing and whether another force could readily replace the contribution. A meaningful role does not have to be sufficient to win the war alone.

Use comparison carefully

Compare Indian defensive service, Algerian soldiers’ experience of gas, Chinese logistical work and Mike Mountain Horse’s individual service. These are different kinds and scales of evidence. Personal courage, large numbers, suffering and remembrance each matter, but none automatically proves the same thing.

Olusoga and the bigger picture

In The World’s War (2014), David Olusoga foregrounds the global, multi-racial character of the conflict and the racial hierarchies shaping service and memory. Test that interpretation through the cases. Do not invent a historian who says empire troops were irrelevant: a narrow battlefield lens here is a classroom comparison, not an attributed quotation.

Vocabulary

  • Significance: importance judged using explicit criteria
  • Decisive: determining an outcome
  • Recognition: acknowledgement, which may differ from contribution

Explain your thinking: What can these four cases establish, and what would require a much wider investigation?

Interpretations

Investigate Olusoga’s interpretation, then test it against a deliberately narrow classroom lens. Only Olusoga’s account is an attributed historian’s interpretation; the comparison lens is explicitly constructed for this exercise. All summaries are paraphrases.

David Olusoga: The World’s War: Forgotten Soldiers of Empire (2014)

Interpretation: The conflict drew in peoples and resources far beyond Europe. Accounts centred only on white European soldiers can obscure imperial service, labour and the unequal treatment of participants.

Evidence and method: A historical synthesis bringing together experiences of African, Asian and other participants, their service and the structures of empire and race.

Context: A modern public historian reassessing a war often remembered through a narrow national or European frame. Evaluate the evidence rather than assuming a historian’s identity determines the argument.

Strength: Changes whose experiences we investigate and encourages us to connect combat, labour and remembrance across empires.

Limitation: Our four classroom cases can test aspects of this interpretation but cannot, alone, establish the scale or military effect of all imperial participation.

A battlefield-only lens: Classroom comparison — not a named historian’s quotation

Interpretation: A classroom claim to test: only a direct effect on battles should count as a meaningful contribution. This framing gives priority to combat and territory gained or defended.

Evidence and method: Focus on battlefield outcomes, unit roles and operational records. It is a question of criteria, not a fabricated published interpretation.

Context: Created for this activity so you can test a restricted definition against the evidence. It is not attributed to Olusoga or an invented opposing historian.

Strength: Prompts precise connections between military action and outcomes, and challenges vague claims that numbers alone prove importance.

Limitation: May ignore the labour and supply systems without which combat could not continue. It also risks treating decisive and meaningful as synonyms.

Argument planning

Did the empire armies make a meaningful contribution to the war effort — why, or why not?

Criteria to consider

  • Manpower at a critical time
  • Holding or gaining positions
  • Sustaining supply and equipment
  • Scale, timing and alternatives
  • Contribution versus recognition

Plan your argument

  1. Set a criterion and make a claim about meaningful contribution.
  2. Use precise evidence from one case.
  3. Explain its military effect, using a causal chain.
  4. Compare with a different case or kind of contribution.
  5. Weigh scale and limits, linking to Olusoga’s interpretation.

Consider a strong counterargument, then explain your qualified final judgement.
