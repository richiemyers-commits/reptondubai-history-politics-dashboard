Inside a motte-and-bailey castle

A Norman timber castle, c.1070

A composite reconstruction of an early Norman timber castle. Buildings and defences varied; many early ditches were dry, and later castles could be rebuilt in stone.

Illustration annotation key

  1. Motte: Earth mound raises the strongpoint.
  2. Timber keep: Tower used for refuge and watch.
  3. Lookout: A high view of the surrounding land.
  4. Timber framing: Oak posts and beams support the tower.
  5. Upper chamber: Accommodation above the stores.
  6. Storage room: Supplies kept inside the keep.
  7. Raised entrance: A stair leads to the tower doorway.
  8. Mound layers: Earth is heaped and compacted.
  9. Summit palisade: Wooden defences protect the motte.
  10. Access bridge: A narrow route links bailey and motte.
  11. Bailey: Enclosed working courtyard.
  12. Great hall: Eating, meetings and household business.
  13. Kitchen: Food prepared away from the main hall.
  14. Stable: Horses need shelter, fodder and care.
  15. Smithy: A smith repairs tools and iron fittings.
  16. Well: Water is essential to the household.
  17. Timber gate: Guards control the entrance.
  18. Palisade: Upright timbers form a defensive fence.
  19. Bank and ditch: Earthworks make approach difficult.
  20. Approach bridge: A crossing over the dry ditch.

Who were the Normans?

Origins, invasion and William’s crown

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Write the heading ‘Who were the Normans?’ and record four facts: where they came from, who led them, the battle they won, and when William became king.
  2. Draw a small two-event timeline: 1066 — Battle of Hastings; 1066 — William crowned king.

Who were the Normans?

The Normans came from Normandy in northern France. Their leader was William, Duke of Normandy. In 1066, William's army invaded England and defeated the Anglo-Saxons at the Battle of Hastings.

The beginning of Norman rule

William was crowned King of England in 1066 and ruled until 1087. Winning a battle did not mean everyone in England accepted his authority: William still needed to control the country and deal with opposition.

Key idea

A conquest is more than winning territory. It also involves keeping control of people, land and government.

Vocabulary

  • Conquest
  • Monarch

Resistance & Rebellion

How William faced opposition, 1067–1071

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Make a four-row timeline for 1067, 1068, 1069 and 1071. For each year, record who rebelled and how William responded.
  2. Underline the change in William's methods from 1067 to 1069.

Early resistance: 1067–1068

In 1067 Edric the Wild rebelled, but William initially ignored this challenge. In 1068 Edwin and Morcar rebelled; William responded by imprisoning rebel leaders and building castles.

The North in 1069

Northern rebels and Vikings challenged Norman rule in 1069. William paid the Vikings to leave and crushed resistance. His forces carried out the Harrying of the North, destroying land and settlements.

Ely in 1071

Hereward resisted William from Ely in 1071. William sent an army to defeat the rebels. Across 1067–1071 his response to rebellion grew more severe.

Key idea

Rebellion means violent resistance against a ruler. Harrying means burning or destroying an area. Both terms help describe the struggle over Norman authority.

Vocabulary

  • Rebellion
  • Harrying
  • Authority

Castles & Control

Fortresses, power and Norman influence

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Draw and label a motte-and-bailey castle, identifying the motte and the bailey.
  2. Make two columns titled ‘Military purpose’ and ‘Symbol of power’. Put at least two reasons for building castles in each, using the chapter reading.

Motte-and-bailey castles

The Normans built many castles. Most of the earliest were motte-and-bailey structures made from wood and earth. The motte was a raised mound; the bailey was the enclosed area below.

Where and why?

Castles housed soldiers, offered defence, and helped Norman rulers control nearby land. The knowledge organiser notes that many were built near borders and marcher areas.

An historian's interpretation

Historian Marc Morris presents castles as both military fortresses and status symbols. A castle could protect Normans while reminding local communities who held power. The organiser notes that some historians estimate more than 500 castles were built.

Key idea

The same change may be useful to one group and threatening to another.

Vocabulary

  • Motte
  • Bailey
  • Fortress

CHAPTER 4 OF 6

Land, Lords & Villeins

The feudal system and Norman society

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Draw a feudal pyramid with King William at the top, earls and knights in the middle, and peasants/villeins at the bottom. Draw arrows showing land going down and loyalty/service going up.
  2. Write definitions of ‘feudal system’, ‘earl’, ‘villein’ and ‘loyalty’.

Land and loyalty

The Normans used the feudal system: the king granted land to powerful followers in return for loyalty and military service. It created a hierarchy, with William at the top, powerful earls and knights below, and peasants at the bottom.

Winners and losers

The organiser states that about 95% of land was given to Normans, having been taken from Anglo-Saxon earls. The system particularly benefited William's followers while many existing landholders lost power.

Life for peasants

A villein was a peasant who farmed land belonging to a lord and was not free to move away. The organiser also links increased taxation with many people becoming villeins.

Other changes

The organiser describes slavery being abolished but women's rights becoming more restricted. These claims are included as presented in the department resource.

Key idea

The impact of the conquest depended on a person's position in society.

Vocabulary

  • Feudal System
  • Earl
  • Villein
  • Loyalty

Explain your thinking: Who gained power from the new system, and who lost it?

CHAPTER 5 OF 6

Church & Government

New leaders, laws and language

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Divide a page into three headings: ‘The Church’, ‘Law and Language’, and ‘Government and Taxation’. Under each, write three clear revision facts.
  2. Write one sentence on a change and one on something that continued after 1066.

Changing the Church

William replaced Archbishop Stigand with the Norman Lanfranc. Many senior Anglo-Saxon clergy were replaced, and monasteries were built or expanded. This helped place influential positions under Norman leadership.

Language and law

The organiser describes Norman French and Latin replacing Old English in important areas of government. It also identifies new legal practices, including trial by combat and the murdrum fine.

The Domesday Book

The Domesday Book was completed in 1086. It surveyed land and resources, helping William understand landholdings and collect taxes more effectively. The organiser notes that taxation increased.

Change and continuity

William did not discard every Anglo-Saxon governing practice: the Normans kept some existing systems but changed the people in charge and introduced new approaches.

Key idea

A government can change by replacing leaders, controlling information and collecting taxes as well as by using armies.

Vocabulary

  • Archbishop
  • Government
  • Domesday Book

Explain your thinking: How far did Norman government change, and what stayed familiar?

CHAPTER 6 OF 6

A Kingdom Transformed?

How historians weigh the consequences

Your written tasks

Write the date and heading in your History exercise book. Read the explanation, then answer these tasks in your own words.

  1. Make a two-column evidence table: ‘Reasons some people suffered’ and ‘Reasons rulers valued Norman changes’. Include at least three examples on each side.
  2. Choose the two strongest pieces of evidence and justify your choices in a sentence each.

The enquiry

The knowledge organiser includes Simon Schama's interpretation of the conquest as a violent and unsettling turning point. Historians use evidence to test interpretations, rather than accepting them automatically.

Evidence of suffering

Rebellions were suppressed; northern communities experienced destruction during the Harrying of the North; many Anglo-Saxon earls lost land; and the conquerors introduced castles that displayed power.

Evidence of organisation and change

Castles could protect territory. The feudal system organised land and service. The Domesday survey helped royal government keep records. Church leadership, language and law also changed.

Different experiences

A Norman knight granted land might describe the conquest differently from an Anglo-Saxon earl who lost an estate or a villein who owed labour to a lord.

Key idea

A strong judgement weighs evidence on both sides and explains who benefited, who suffered, and how significant the changes were.

Vocabulary

  • Interpretation
  • Consequence
  • Continuity

Explain your thinking: Which change seems most significant, and what evidence supports that view?
